🏆 SECTION 1 & 2: Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN) & BPTT Derivation

Marks: 8 to 10 Marks | Frequency: 3x in Univ Papers (100% Guaranteed)

1.1 Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) Formulation

Standard feedforward networks assume all inputs and outputs are independent. RNNs introduce cyclical feedback loops, maintaining an internal hidden state $h_t$ that acts as a sequential memory buffer. * Forward State Update Equation: $$h_t = \tanh(W_{hh} h_{t-1} + W_{xh} x_t + b_h)$$ * Output Prediction Equation: $$\hat{y}_t = \text{softmax}(W_{hy} h_t + b_y)$$ * Where: * $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$: Input vector at time step $t$. * $h_t \in \mathbb{R}^h$: Hidden state vector at time step $t$. * $W_{xh}$: Input-to-hidden weight matrix. * $W_{hh}$: Hidden-to-hidden recurrence weight matrix. * $W_{hy}$: Hidden-to-output weight matrix.

2.1 Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT) Mathematical Derivation

During training, the recurrent network is unrolled across time steps $t = 1 \dots T$. The total sequence loss is the sum of losses at all time steps: $$\mathcal{L} = \sum_{t=1}^T \mathcal{L}_t$$ To calculate the gradient of loss $\mathcal{L}_t$ with respect to the recurrent weight matrix $W_{hh}$, we apply the multivariable chain rule through hidden states $h_t, h_{t-1}, \dots, h_1$: $$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_t}{\partial W_{hh}} = \sum_{k=1}^t \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_t}{\partial \hat{y}_t} \frac{\partial \hat{y}_t}{\partial h_t} \left( \frac{\partial h_t}{\partial h_k} \right) \frac{\partial h_k}{\partial W_{hh}}$$ The temporal Jacobian chain product term is: $$\frac{\partial h_t}{\partial h_k} = \prod_{j=k+1}^t \frac{\partial h_j}{\partial h_{j-1}} = \prod_{j=k+1}^t \text{diag}(1 - h_j^2) \cdot W_{hh}^T$$

2.2 Why Vanishing and Exploding Gradients Occur in Simple RNNs

Notice the repeated matrix multiplication of recurrent weight transpose $W_{hh}^T$ over $t - k$ time steps: 1. Vanishing Gradient ($\lambda_{\max}(W_{hh}) < 1$): * If the largest eigenvalue of $W_{hh}$ is less than 1, or because $\tanh'(z) = 1 - h^2 \le 1$, raising a fraction to the $(t - k)^{\text{th}}$ power decays exponentially to zero ($0.5^{20} \approx 9.5 \times 10^{-7}$). * Consequence: Gradients from long-distance time steps vanish, meaning the network cannot learn long-term temporal dependencies (e.g., subject-verb agreement across 20 words). 2. Exploding Gradient ($\lambda_{\max}(W_{hh}) > 1$): * If eigenvalues exceed 1, gradients grow exponentially, causing numerical overflow (NaNs) and wild parameter oscillation. * Mitigation for Exploding Gradient: Gradient Clipping: $$\text{if } \|\mathbf{g}\| > \theta \implies \mathbf{g} \leftarrow \theta \frac{\mathbf{g}}{\|\mathbf{g}\|}$$ ---

🏆 SECTION 3: Bidirectional RNNs (BiRNN) for Named Entity Recognition (NER)

Marks: 7 Marks | Frequency: 1x in Univ Papers (May 2024)

3.1 The Limitation of Unidirectional RNNs

A standard forward RNN reads sequential data in one direction ($x_1 \to x_t$). At time step $t$, the hidden state $h_t$ only contains past context ($x_1 \dots x_{t-1}$). However, in human language and NLP, understanding the true meaning of a word frequently depends on future context ($x_{t+1} \dots x_T$).

3.2 Bidirectional Architecture

A Bidirectional RNN (Schuster & Paliwal, 1997) processes the sequence with two separate hidden layers: 1. Forward RNN: Processes tokens from past to future ($t = 1 \to T$): $$\overrightarrow{h}_t = \tanh(W_{x\overrightarrow{h}} x_t + W_{\overrightarrow{h}\overrightarrow{h}} \overrightarrow{h}_{t-1} + b_{\overrightarrow{h}})$$ 2. Backward RNN: Processes tokens in reverse from future to past ($t = T \to 1$): $$\overleftarrow{h}_t = \tanh(W_{x\overleftarrow{h}} x_t + W_{\overleftarrow{h}\overleftarrow{h}} \overleftarrow{h}_{t+1} + b_{\overleftarrow{h}})$$ 3. Combined Output Representation: Concatenates both contextual states: $$h_t = \left[ \overrightarrow{h}_t \,;\, \overleftarrow{h}_t \right]$$

3.3 How Bidirectional RNN Solves Named Entity Recognition (NER)

Consider the polysemous token "Apple" in two distinct sentences: Sentence A: "Apple announced its revolutionary quantum neural processor yesterday in California."* Sentence B: "Apple tastes crisp and refreshing when eaten cold from the orchard."* When a unidirectional RNN encounters the first token "Apple", it has zero prior words ($h_0 = 0$). It cannot determine whether "Apple" is an Organization (ORG) or a Fruit (FOOD). How BiRNN Resolves This: The backward RNN reads from right to left, processing words like "processor / California" (Sentence A) and "eaten / orchard"* (Sentence B). When concatenated, the combined representation $h_t$ at token "Apple" provides rich bidirectional semantic clues, enabling the classification head to classify Sentence A as B-ORG and Sentence B as O (Other/Fruit). ---

🏆 SECTION 4: Encoder-Decoder Sequence-to-Sequence (Seq2Seq) Architectures

Marks: 8 to 10 Marks | Probability: High Priority (75%)

4.1 Motivation & Applications

Standard RNNs require input and output sequences to have identical lengths. Many critical NLP tasks involve variable-length input and output mappings: * Machine Translation (e.g., English to German). * Text Summarization (Long paragraph to 1 sentence). * Conversational AI & Chatbots (User query to bot response).

4.2 Architectural Components (Sutskever et al., 2014)

1. The Encoder: * An RNN/LSTM that reads the input token sequence $X = (x_1, x_2, \dots, x_{T_x})$. * Discards intermediate outputs and outputs the final hidden state as a fixed-dimensional Context Vector $C = h_{T_x}$, which acts as a compressed semantic summary of the entire source sentence. 2. The Decoder: * An RNN/LSTM initialized with hidden state $s_0 = C$. * Generates target tokens $Y = (y_1, y_2, \dots, y_{T_y})$ sequentially: $$s_t = f(s_{t-1}, y_{t-1}, C), \quad P(y_t \mid y_{ is emitted.

4.3 Training & Inference Techniques

Teacher Forcing (Training): During training, instead of feeding the decoder's own predicted token $\hat{y}_{t-1}$ to the next step, the ground-truth target token $y_{t-1}^$ is supplied. This prevents early prediction errors from cascading and destabilizing gradient convergence. * Greedy Search vs. Beam Search (Inference): Greedy Search:* Selects the single highest-probability token at each step. Fast but myopic; misses globally optimal sentences. Beam Search:* Maintains a beam of top-$B$ most probable candidate sequences at each step, pruning low-scoring branches to find the global optimum. * The Information Bottleneck & Attention: For sentences longer than 20 words, compressing an entire paragraph into a single fixed vector $C$ causes severe information loss. This bottleneck led to Bahdanau's Attention Mechanism, which computes dynamic context vectors $c_t = \sum \alpha_{ti} h_i$. ---

🏆 SECTION 5: Long Short-Term Memory Networks (LSTM)

Marks: 8 to 10 Marks | Frequency: 2x in Univ Papers (100% Guaranteed)

5.1 The Constant Error Carousel (CEC) & Cell State

Hochreiter & Schmidhuber (1997) solved the vanishing gradient problem by introducing: 1. The Cell State ($C_t$): An internal highway conveyor belt that runs straight down the entire recurrent chain, undergoing only linear additive updates. 2. Three Multiplicative Gating Mechanisms: Regulated by sigmoid activations ($\sigma \in [0, 1]$) to conditionally add or remove information.

5.2 The 4 Full Mathematical LSTM Equations

Given input $x_t$ and previous hidden state $h_{t-1}$: 1. Forget Gate ($f_t$): Decides what information to discard from the previous cell state: $$f_t = \sigma\left( W_f \cdot [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_f \right)$$ * $\sigma = 0$: Completely forget this information. * $\sigma = 1$: Completely preserve this information. 2. Input Gate ($i_t$) & Candidate State ($\tilde{C}_t$): Decides what new information to store in cell state: $$i_t = \sigma\left( W_i \cdot [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_i \right)$$ $$\tilde{C}_t = \tanh\left( W_c \cdot [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_c \right)$$ 3. Cell State Update ($C_t$): Linearly combines old cell memory with new candidate memory: $$C_t = f_t \odot C_{t-1} + i_t \odot \tilde{C}_t$$ (The additive $\odot$ operation allows gradients to flow across hundreds of time steps without decaying!). 4. Output Gate ($o_t$) & Hidden State ($h_t$): Decides what parts of cell state to reveal as output: $$o_t = \sigma\left( W_o \cdot [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_o \right)$$ $$h_t = o_t \odot \tanh(C_t)$$ ---

🏆 SECTION 6: Advanced RNN: LSTM vs. Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU)

Marks: 7 to 8 Marks | Frequency: 2x in Univ Papers (May 2024, Nov 2023)

6.1 Gated Recurrent Unit (Cho et al., 2014)

The GRU simplifies the LSTM by merging the cell state and hidden state into a single state $h_t$, and reducing the gate count from 3 down to 2: 1. Reset Gate ($r_t$): Determines how much of past memory to forget: $$r_t = \sigma(W_r \cdot [h_{t-1}, x_t])$$ 2. Update Gate ($z_t$): Simultaneously controls forgetting and accepting new input: $$z_t = \sigma(W_z \cdot [h_{t-1}, x_t])$$ 3. Candidate Hidden State ($\tilde{h}_t$): $$\tilde{h}_t = \tanh(W \cdot [r_t \odot h_{t-1}, x_t])$$ 4. Final Hidden State Update: $$h_t = (1 - z_t) \odot h_{t-1} + z_t \odot \tilde{h}_t$$

6.2 Comprehensive 8-Point Distinction: LSTM vs. GRU

Comparison ParameterLong Short-Term Memory (LSTM)Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU)
1. Number of Gates3 Gates (Forget, Input, Output)2 Gates (Reset, Update)
2. Internal States2 States: Separate Cell State $C_t$ & Hidden State $h_t$1 State: Only Hidden State $h_t$
3. Update ControlIndependent gates for forgetting ($f_t$) and input ($i_t$)Coupled update gate: $(1 - z_t)$ forgets while $z_t$ inputs
4. Parameter CountHigher: $4 \times (d_h^2 + d_h d_x + d_h)$25% Fewer Parameters: $3 \times (d_h^2 + d_h d_x + d_h)$
5. Computational SpeedSlower per epoch due to more matrix multiplicationsFaster training & inference; computationally efficient
6. Memory FootprintHigher memory required to cache statesLower memory required
7. Dataset SuitabilityExcels on very large datasets and long sequences ($>100$ tokens)Excels on smaller to medium datasets; resists overfitting
8. PerformanceMarginally superior expressive power on complex tasksComparable performance with significantly less compute

🏆 SECTION 7: Introduction to Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)

Marks: 8 to 10 Marks | Frequency: 1x in Univ Papers (Nov 2023)

7.1 What are Generative Models?

* Discriminative Models: Model the conditional probability $P(Y \mid X)$ (e.g., given an image, predict if it's a cat or dog). * Generative Models: Model the joint probability distribution $P(X)$ or $P(X, Y)$ to generate brand new synthetic data samples that resemble the true training distribution.

7.2 GAN Architecture (Ian Goodfellow et al., 2014)

A GAN consists of two competing sub-networks locked in a game-theoretic contest: 1. Generator ($G$): * Takes a random latent noise vector $z \sim p_z(z)$ (Gaussian or Uniform). * Maps it to data space $G(z)$ to synthesize realistic images/text that fool the discriminator. 2. Discriminator ($D$): * A binary classifier that receives either real data $x \sim p_{\text{data}}$ or fake data $G(z)$. * Outputs probability $D(x) \in [0, 1]$ indicating whether the sample is real ($1$) or fake ($0$).

7.3 The Two-Player Minimax Game Objective Function

$$\min_G \max_D V(D, G) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}(x)}\left[ \log D(x) \right] + \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p_z(z)}\left[ \log (1 - D(G(z))) \right]$$ * Discriminator's Goal ($\max_D$): Wants $D(x) \to 1$ and $D(G(z)) \to 0$, maximizing $V(D,G)$. * Generator's Goal ($\min_G$): Wants $D(G(z)) \to 1$ so that $\log(1 - D(G(z))) \to -\infty$, minimizing $V(D,G)$. * Nash Equilibrium: The ideal equilibrium is reached when the generator reproduces the true data distribution $p_g = p_{\text{data}}$, and the discriminator outputs $D(x) = \frac{1}{2}$ for all inputs (pure random guess).
